9. Ridge vent

Canonical: ridge vent
Aliases: continuous ridge vent (manufacturer), shingle-over ridge vent
(manufacturer), ridge vent strip (field), roll vent (field — specifically the
flexible rolled type), peak vent (field), aluminum ridge vent (field — the older
style)

What it is: Continuous vent running along the roof peak over a cut slot in the
deck, letting air out the full length of the ridge.

Function: Exhaust for the attic, distributed along the ridge instead of at
points. Requires soffit intake to do anything.

Three distinct types, and this is the part worth getting right:
1. Shingle-over — rigid, nailed down, then covered with hip-and-ridge cap
   shingles so the fasteners are hidden. The current default.
2. Aluminum / metal exposed — the older style, common in the 80s and 90s.
   Fasteners go through the surface and into the deck, so they're exposed and rely
   on sealant that needs re-application. Comes in 8- or 10-foot sections.
3. Roll vent — flexible corrugated strip, rolled out and shingled over. Easy on
   long ridges but generally lower net free area than rigid.

What varies (go determine):
- Ridge length available (short or cut-up ridgelines are why some roofs still get
  static vents instead).
- Whether it has an external baffle — the baffle is what keeps wind-driven rain
  from pushing back over the ridge. Worth checking on any ridge vent you're
  matching.
- Roof covering — metal roofs take a vented ridge closure or a vented ridge cap
  matched to the panel profile, not a shingle-over product.

Replacement direction: Match type to covering first. On a shingle roof, a
shingle-over vent is the like-for-like or the upgrade from aluminum. Metal roofs
need a matched metal system.

Failure modes: Aluminum vents get stepped on, blow off in high wind, and leak
at the exposed fasteners once the sealant lets go. Shingle-over units fail mostly
from bad install — slot cut wrong, or not run far enough.

20-year note: Aluminum was common through the 80s and 90s and is what you'll
find on older roofs; shingle-over has largely replaced it on re-roofs.

Confidence: HIGH

10. Off-ridge vent

Canonical: off-ridge vent
Aliases: low-profile vent (manufacturer), slant vent (field)

What it is: A long, thin static vent placed over a cut slot near but not at the
ridge. Functionally between a box vent and a ridge vent.

Function: Exhaust where the ridge itself can't be used — short ridgelines,
hips, or where the ridge is interrupted.

Why it needs its own entry: People confuse these with box vents constantly.
The distinguishing observation is shape: long and thin rather than square.

Replacement direction: Match the slot length and profile.

Confidence: MEDIUM. I'd want your read on how often you actually see these
around here versus how often the term gets used loosely for a box vent.

11. Powered attic ventilator

Canonical: powered attic ventilator
Aliases: power vent (field), PAV (manufacturer/inspection), attic fan (field —
loose, see note), power attic vent (manufacturer), roof-mount attic fan (field)

TERM COLLISION — flag this one. "Attic fan" gets used for at least three
different things: a roof-mounted powered attic ventilator, a gable-mounted fan, and
a whole-house fan (which is a completely different appliance that pulls air from
the living space). The entry should disambiguate rather than assume.

What it is: Electric fan in a domed housing on a flanged base, controlled by a
thermostat and often a humidistat.

Function: Mechanically exhausts hot attic air rather than relying on convection.

What varies (go determine):
- Throat/hole size and base dimensions. Standard bases are around 24" x 24", but
  measure.
- Control type: thermostat only, or thermostat plus humidistat.
- Roof pitch.
- Whether it's hardwired or plugged into a nearby receptacle.

Replacement direction: Same throat size. Two useful substitution paths:
- These are commonly sold as a replacement for a turbine or a roof pot, so a
  turbine hole often takes a power vent directly.
- Going the other way, a dead power vent is frequently replaced with a static vent
  or a solar unit rather than another electric one.
- Thermostat/humidistat controls are replaceable separately — a fan that won't come
  on is often a control, not a motor.

Failure modes: Motors die, and they die early — they're air-cooled and moving
120°F+ air. Noise. Thermal overload trips. The bigger critique in the trade is that
a PAV without adequate soffit intake pulls conditioned air out of the living space,
which costs more than the attic cooling saves.

Confidence: HIGH on the hardware. The performance criticism is contested — real
disagreement in the trade, not settled fact. Present it as a consideration, not a
verdict.

12. Solar attic fan

Canonical: solar attic fan
Aliases: solar powered attic ventilator (manufacturer), solar attic vent
(manufacturer), solar fan (field)

What it is: Attic exhaust fan with an integrated or separate photovoltaic panel.
No wiring to the house.

Function: Same as a PAV, without the electrical run or the power draw.

What varies (go determine):
- Wattage — 20W through 60W are common residential sizes, and it drives the CFM and
  the attic square footage it covers.
- Whether the panel is integrated on the housing or remote-mounted.
- Panel tilt adjustability, which matters on a roof plane facing the wrong way.
- Whether it has a thermostat, humidistat, or both.
- Whether there's a hybrid/night adapter.

Replacement direction: Common upgrade path from a dead electric PAV, since it
reuses the hole and drops the wiring problem. Match throat size.

Failure modes: Panel degradation, controller failure, and the same
intake-balance critique as a PAV. Hail is a live concern in your territory — some
units advertise hail resistance and Texas windstorm documentation specifically,
which is worth noting in the entry since that's a real regional differentiator.

Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH. Product landscape moves fast here, so I'd keep the
entry about the category and avoid naming brands.

13. Skylight

Canonical: skylight
Aliases: roof window (manufacturer — technically a different product, but used
loosely), sky light (field), fixed skylight / venting skylight (manufacturer)

What it is: Glazed unit in a framed opening in the roof.

The critical distinction — mount type. This is what someone needs to determine
before they can order anything:
1. Deck mount — sits directly on the roof deck, low profile, uses a
   manufacturer flashing kit matched to the covering. Not suitable for low slopes.
2. Curb mount — sits on a wood curb built up above the roof plane. Standard for
   flat and low-slope, and the usual choice for replacements because you can often
   reuse the existing curb.
3. Self-flashing — flashing integrated into the frame. Note: "self-flashing" is
   sometimes used as another name for deck mount, which is a genuine source of
   confusion. Generally limited to shingle roofs at 3:12 or steeper.

What varies (go determine):
- Mount type (above).
- Rough opening dimensions.
- Roof covering — flashing kits are specific to shingle, high-profile tile, or
  metal, and they are not interchangeable.
- Roof pitch.
- Fixed vs. venting, and if venting, manual / electric / solar.

Replacement direction: On a curb-mount replacement you can often keep the curb
and change only the unit, which is a much smaller job. Deck-mount replacements
need the correct flashing kit for the covering, and that kit is a separate part
number from the skylight.

Confidence: HIGH on the mount distinction. The "self-flashing = deck mount"
ambiguity is real and belongs in the entry.

14. Solar tube

Canonical: tubular skylight
Aliases: solar tube (field), sun tunnel (manufacturer — VELUX term), sun tube
(field), light tube (manufacturer), Solatube (brand, heavily genericized),
tubular daylighting device (manufacturer)

What it is: Reflective tube running from a roof dome down to a ceiling diffuser.

Function: Daylight into interior spaces with no line of sight to the sky —
hallways, baths, closets. Cheaper and less invasive than a skylight.

What varies (go determine):
- Tube diameter. 10" and 14" are standard and fit between 16"-on-center framing;
  22" exists for larger rooms.
- Roof covering — flashing is specific to shingle vs. tile vs. metal, and some
  models ship without a flashing kit at all.
- Rigid vs. flexible tube.
- Whether there's a solar night light or venting option.

Replacement direction: Match diameter and covering. The roof-side dome is often
replaceable on its own when the acrylic clouds or cracks, which is a much smaller
repair than the whole assembly.

Failure modes: Acrylic dome degrades and yellows in UV; seal at the flashing;
condensation in the tube.

Confidence: HIGH

15. Abandoned penetration / swamp cooler curb

Canonical: abandoned roof penetration
Aliases: capped penetration (field), dead vent (field), swamp cooler curb
(field), evaporative cooler curb (field/manufacturer), roof curb (manufacturer),
plenum opening (field)

REGIONAL FLAG — decide whether this belongs in your list at all. Swamp coolers
are a dry-climate appliance. They're extremely common in Denver, Utah, Phoenix, and
West Texas, and much less so in Central Texas humidity. You'll know whether you
actually run into these. If not, the entry is still worth having under the broader
"abandoned penetration" heading, because you definitely run into those.

What it is: An opening left in the deck where equipment used to be — a removed
cooler, a dead attic fan, an old flue, a relocated vent.

Function: None, and that's the point. Someone finds a mystery patch, a rusted
curb, or a hump under the shingles and needs to know what it was and what to do.

What varies (go determine):
- Whether the deck under it is sound. Rot around an abandoned penetration is common
  and is the actual issue.
- Opening dimensions.
- Whether anything is still connected below — wiring, water line, ductwork.

Replacement direction: Options are usually: patch the deck with new sheathing
and shingle over, or reuse the opening for something else. A removed cooler opening
commonly becomes a skylight, solar tube, or attic fan, since the hole is already
there and framed. That's a genuinely useful thing for the app to say.

Confidence: MEDIUM on the swamp cooler specifics for your area. HIGH on the
general abandoned-penetration case.

16. Small mast and line penetrations

Canonical: mast and line penetration
Aliases: satellite mast (field), dish mount (field), lineset penetration
(field), refrigerant line penetration (manufacturer), conduit penetration (field),
electrical mast (field), condensate line penetration (field)

What it is: The small holes — antenna and satellite mounts, HVAC refrigerant
linesets, condensate lines, conduit, mini-split penetrations.

Function: Individually minor, collectively a common leak source, and frequently
flashed badly or not at all — just sealant.

What varies (go determine):
- OD of whatever is passing through.
- Whether it's still in service (abandoned dish mounts are everywhere).
- Whether the penetration is at an angle, which most standard boots don't handle.

Replacement direction: This is the main use case for retrofit and split
flashings from v1 entry #2 — you can't disconnect a live lineset or a mast to slide
a boot down it. Cut-to-fit EPDM or silicone boots cover a wide OD range and are the
usual answer.

Note: Mini-split installs put small penetrations in roofs and walls that
weren't there twenty years ago. That's a real 20-year change and one you'll see
more of.

Confidence: MEDIUM. This entry is a catch-all and may want to split once you
see how people actually search for these.
