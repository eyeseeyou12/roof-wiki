1. Pipe boot

Canonical: pipe boot
Aliases: pipe jack (field), roof jack (field), vent boot (field), plumbing vent
boot (field), plumbing vent flashing (manufacturer), vent stack flashing
(manufacturer), boot flashing (manufacturer), neoprene boot (field), lead boot
(field — specifically the lead-flashing version)

What it is: Flanged base that laces into the shingles, with a collar that seals
around a plumbing vent pipe where it exits the roof.

Function: Sewer gas vents to the outside; the boot keeps water from running down
the outside of the pipe into the deck.

What varies (go determine):
- Pipe diameter. Sizes are nominal, not the measurement you take off the outside
  of the pipe. Most residential PVC vents are 2", 3", or 4".
- Collar material: rubber/neoprene, silicone, or lead.
- Base material: aluminum, galvanized, or plastic.
- Roof pitch — some bases are pitch-specific.

Replacement direction: Same nominal pipe size, base compatible with the roof
covering. Material is an upgrade decision, not a compatibility one — lead and
silicone outlast rubber. If the pipe can't be lifted or disconnected, you need a
retrofit or split type rather than a standard boot.

Failure modes: Rubber collar cracks and splits from UV, typically well before
the shingles are done. Chalking, curling, rust at the flange, exposed nail heads.
Sealant over a split collar is a delay, not a repair.

Confidence: HIGH

2. Split boot / retrofit boot

Canonical: retrofit pipe boot
Aliases: split boot (field), two-piece flashing (manufacturer), retrofit pipe
flashing (manufacturer), zipper boot (field)

IMPORTANT — this term is ambiguous in the field and the app needs to handle it.
"Split boot" means two different things depending on who's talking:
1. A boot whose collar has split — a failure condition.
2. A two-piece product that wraps around an existing pipe so you can flash it
   without pulling the pipe or tearing off shingles.

Someone searching "split boot" could mean either. The entry should ask which.

What it is: Two-piece or slit flashing that wraps around a pipe already in place
and locks closed.

Function: Flash or re-flash a penetration where the pipe can't be lifted —
repairs, storm work, masts, HVAC stacks.

What varies: Pipe OD (these are usually cut-to-fit within a range), roof
covering, whether the base tucks under shingles.

Replacement direction: Also the substitute *for* a standard boot when access is
the constraint. Worth noting the competing repair: a universal rubber collar that
slips over an existing intact metal or lead base, which is a smaller job than
replacing anything.

Confidence: HIGH on the product. MEDIUM on how commonly "split boot" is used
for each of the two meanings around here — your call.

3. Static roof vent

Canonical: static roof vent
Aliases: box vent (field), turtle vent (field), turtle back (field), louver vent
(manufacturer), low profile vent (manufacturer), flat vent (field), slant back
(manufacturer), pan vent (manufacturer), 750 (field — Lomanco model number that
became a generic name), 750 vent (field)

What it is: Passive exhaust vent over a cut opening near the ridge. No moving
parts.

Function: Lets hot, moist attic air out. Pairs with soffit intake — exhaust
without intake doesn't do much.

What varies (go determine):
- Throat/hole size — this is the number that matters, not the outside dimensions.
  12" is common.
- Profile: slant back vs. pan/dome style.
- Material: galvanized, aluminum, or plastic.
- Color.

Replacement direction: Any static vent with the same throat size and a
compatible profile. Colors and brands are interchangeable. This is one of the
easier swaps on a roof.

20-year note: Still the default and cheapest option, but ridge vent has taken
a lot of share on new installs and re-roofs.

Confidence: HIGH. The 750 alias is worth confirming with your suppliers — it's
regional how strongly that model name has genericized.

4. Turbine vent

Canonical: turbine vent
Aliases: whirlybird (field), wind turbine (field), spinner (field), turbine
(field)

What it is: Wind-driven exhaust vent with a spinning head on a base flange.

Function: Same job as a static vent, with wind-assisted draw.

What varies (go determine): Throat size (12" and 14" are the common residential
sizes), base pitch, material.

Replacement direction: Like-for-like, OR — and this is the useful part — there
are static replacement heads made to drop onto an existing turbine base and throat
size. That's the common move when a turbine is noisy, seized, or the homeowner
doesn't want another one. Match the throat size.

Failure modes: Bearings seize, gets noisy, head wobbles or gets blown off in
high wind, which leaves an open hole.

20-year note: Falling out of favor, partly on appearance. Still very common on
existing Central Texas roofs.

Confidence: HIGH

5. B-vent roof penetration (assembly)

This is one penetration made of three separate parts people confuse for each other.
Worth an entry that names all three and links out.

Canonical: Type B gas vent roof penetration
Aliases: B-vent (field/manufacturer), double-wall flue (field), furnace flue
(field), gas flue (field), heat stack (field — see note)

HEAT STACK — FLAG. Usage is inconsistent. Heard for the flue penetration as a
whole and for the metal collar around it. Regional. Needs your call on what it
should resolve to, and the entry should probably say the term is ambiguous rather
than pick one.

The three parts, bottom to top:
1. Flashing cone (base flashing, flashing boot, roof jack) — laces into the
   shingles, metal cone around the pipe.
2. Storm collar — the ring clamped to the pipe above the cone. Acts as an
   umbrella over the joint between cone and pipe. Sealed with high-temp sealant.
3. Rain cap / flue cap — see next entry.

What varies (go determine):
- Pipe OD, and whether it's single-wall or B-vent (double-wall). These are sized
  differently and the parts are not interchangeable between them.
- Roof pitch — flashings come in standard and steep-roof versions.
- Brand. B-vent is a listed system; manufacturers say don't mix brands on the pipe
  and cap.

Replacement direction: Match wall type first, then diameter, then pitch. Cap
should match the pipe manufacturer where possible.

Failure modes: Leaks at the cone neck and at the storm collar; wind flex cracks
the sealant. Water runs down between the inner and outer wall, so it can show up
far from the penetration. This is a common re-roof line item — cap, collar, and
flashing usually get replaced together with the roof.

Confidence: HIGH on the parts. THIN on "heat stack" — that's yours.

6. Rain cap / flue cap

Canonical: vent cap
Aliases: rain cap (field/manufacturer), flue cap (field), chimney cap (field —
loosely, and often wrong), vent cap (manufacturer), draft cap (field)

What it is: Cap on top of a flue that keeps rain out while letting exhaust
escape.

Function: Rain and debris exclusion, and on some designs, draft protection.

What varies (go determine):
- Flue diameter.
- Single-wall vs. B-vent — different cap types, not interchangeable.
- Whether the appliance is a Category I natural-draft unit or high-efficiency
  (high-efficiency units usually vent PVC out a sidewall, not a roof flue).

Replacement direction: Same wall type, same diameter, rated for the appliance
category. Same-brand as the pipe is the safe answer for B-vent.

Confidence: HIGH. Worth splitting from #5 or folding into it — your call once
you see how people search it.

7. Bathroom exhaust roof termination

Canonical: bath exhaust roof vent
Aliases: bathroom vent (field), bath fan vent (field), exhaust vent cap
(manufacturer), goose neck (field — for the hooded style), roof cap (manufacturer)

What it is: Hooded or louvered cap where a bath fan duct terminates through the
roof.

Function: Dumps humid air outside the envelope. Terminating into the attic
instead of through the roof is a common defect and a moisture problem.

What varies (go determine): Duct diameter (4" typical), whether there's a
damper, hood style.

Replacement direction: Match duct size. Screened is fine here — bath vents may
be screened. A dryer termination can serve a bath fan, but not the reverse.

Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH

8. Dryer vent roof termination

Canonical: dryer exhaust roof vent
Aliases: dryer vent (field), dryer jack (field/brand — DryerJack has partly
genericized), dryer roof cap (manufacturer)

What it is: Termination cap for a clothes dryer duct through the roof.

Function: Exhausts lint-laden air. Different requirements from a bath vent.

What varies (go determine): Duct diameter (4"), damper type, roof pitch.

Replacement direction: Must have a backdraft damper. Must not have a screen
— lint clogs it and that's a fire issue. This is the key distinction from a bath
vent and it's the kind of thing worth calling out in the entry, because people swap
these interchangeably and shouldn't. A bath vent cap is not an acceptable dryer
termination if it's screened.

Confidence: HIGH on the requirements. Cite IRC M1502.3 if you want it in the
entry.

Notes on sourcing

Everything above is assembled from manufacturer catalogs, trade forums, code
references, and roofing trade sites. Manufacturer sources are reliable on what
exists now; the field aliases and the 20-year picture come from trade discussion
and are the parts most worth your correction. The regional slang in particular
(750, heat stack, turtle) is exactly where a Central Texas contractor knows more
than any published source.
